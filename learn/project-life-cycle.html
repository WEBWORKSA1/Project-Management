The Project Life Cycle: 5 Phases Explained

What happens in each phase, what you should produce, and the questions to answer before moving on.

Updated 2026-10-31 · 8 min read · By the Project.Management editorial team

The project life cycle is the series of phases a project passes through from idea to completion. Naming the phases matters because each one needs different skills, involves different people and ends with a decision: continue, change course or stop. Organisations that formalise these decisions — called phase gates or stage gates — cancel bad projects earlier and waste less money.

Phase 1: Initiation

The goal of initiation is to decide whether the project should exist at all, and if so, to define it well enough to plan.

  • Clarify the problem or opportunity and the business case.
  • Identify the sponsor and key stakeholders.
  • Define high-level scope, objectives and success criteria.
  • Estimate order-of-magnitude cost and duration.
  • Identify major risks and constraints.

Key deliverable: a signed project charter.

Gate question

Is the expected value worth the cost and risk — and do we have a sponsor willing to own it?

Phase 2: Planning

Planning turns the charter into a roadmap the team can execute. The depth of planning depends on your methodology: a construction project may plan in detail for months, while an agile team plans a release roadmap and then details only the next sprint.

  • Build a work breakdown structure.
  • Estimate effort and duration (try PERT).
  • Sequence work and find the critical path.
  • Create the budget and resource plan.
  • Plan risk, quality, communication and procurement.
  • Agree roles with a RACI matrix.

Key deliverables: project management plan, baseline schedule and budget, risk register.

Gate question

Is the plan realistic, resourced and agreed by the people who will do the work?

Phase 3: Execution

Execution is where most of the budget is spent. The project manager's focus shifts from planning to leading: coordinating the team, managing suppliers, keeping stakeholders informed and protecting the team from distraction.

  • Kick off the project and onboard the team.
  • Assign and coordinate work.
  • Manage quality and supplier delivery.
  • Run the communication plan.

Phase 4: Monitoring and controlling

This phase runs in parallel with execution. It compares actual progress with the baseline and takes corrective action.

  • Track schedule and cost (use earned value for objective numbers).
  • Review and update risks and issues weekly.
  • Run change control: every scope change is assessed for impact on time, cost and risk before approval.
  • Report status — a one-page status report with RAG ratings works for most audiences.
Status reports should answer three questions: Where are we? What has changed? What do you need from me?

Phase 5: Closure

Closure is the phase most often skipped — and the one that stops organisations repeating the same mistakes.

  • Confirm acceptance of deliverables against success criteria.
  • Hand over to operations with documentation and training.
  • Close contracts and release people and budget.
  • Run a lessons-learned session (template).
  • Celebrate the team.

Life cycle models

ModelHow it worksBest for
PredictivePhases run once, in sequenceStable scope, high cost of change
IterativeRepeat phases to refine the solutionComplex solutions needing feedback
IncrementalDeliver usable pieces in sequenceEarly value delivery
Adaptive (agile)Short iterative and incremental cyclesHigh uncertainty
HybridMix of the aboveMost real-world organisations
