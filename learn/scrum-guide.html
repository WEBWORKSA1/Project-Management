Scrum Explained

Everything you need to run your first sprint — and the mistakes to avoid in your tenth.

Updated 2026-10-31 · 10 min read · By the Project.Management editorial team

Scrum is a lightweight framework for teams solving complex problems. Work is done in fixed-length iterations called sprints, each producing a usable increment. Scrum is deliberately incomplete: it defines a small set of accountabilities, events and artifacts and leaves the rest — engineering practices, estimation techniques, tools — to the team. That simplicity is why it is the most widely used agile framework, and also why it is easy to do badly.

The three pillars

Scrum rests on empiricism — making decisions based on what is observed rather than what was predicted:

  • Transparency: the work and progress are visible to everyone who needs to see them.
  • Inspection: the team frequently checks progress toward goals.
  • Adaptation: when something is off track, the team adjusts as soon as possible.

The three accountabilities

AccountabilityOwnsDoes not
Product OwnerMaximising the value of the product; ordering the Product Backlog; the Product GoalTell developers how to do the work
Scrum MasterThe team's effectiveness; coaching Scrum; removing impedimentsAssign tasks or act as team boss
DevelopersCreating a usable increment each sprint; the Sprint Backlog; qualityWait to be told what to do

A Scrum team is typically ten people or fewer, cross-functional and self-managing.

The five events

  1. The Sprint — the container for all other events; one month or less, usually two weeks.
  2. Sprint Planning — the team agrees why the sprint is valuable (Sprint Goal), what can be done and how. Time-box: up to 8 hours for a one-month sprint.
  3. Daily Scrum — 15 minutes for developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the plan for the next day.
  4. Sprint Review — the team presents results to stakeholders and discusses what to do next. It is a working session, not a slide show.
  5. Sprint Retrospective — the team inspects how it worked and commits to improvements.

The three artifacts and their commitments

  • Product Backlog → committed to the Product Goal. An ordered list of everything that might be needed.
  • Sprint Backlog → committed to the Sprint Goal. The items selected for the sprint plus the plan to deliver them.
  • Increment → committed to the Definition of Done. A usable step toward the Product Goal that meets the quality bar.

Estimation and velocity

Scrum does not prescribe estimation, but many teams size backlog items in story points — a relative measure of effort, complexity and uncertainty — often using planning poker with a Fibonacci-like scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13). Velocity is the number of points completed per sprint; averaged over several sprints, it helps forecast how much the team can take on and when a release might be done. Try the sprint capacity planner.

Common anti-patterns

  • Status-meeting Daily Scrum: people report to a manager instead of coordinating with each other.
  • No Sprint Goal: the sprint becomes a to-do list with no unifying purpose.
  • Carry-over every sprint: a sign of over-commitment or items that are too large.
  • Weak Definition of Done: "done except testing" creates hidden work.
  • Product Owner by committee: slow, conflicting decisions.

Getting started

Start with a two-week sprint, a short Product Backlog with the top ten items refined, a clear Definition of Done and a simple board — physical or digital. Run three sprints before changing the framework; most early problems are signs of organisational impediments that Scrum is designed to expose. For formal training, compare Scrum certifications.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a sprint be?

One month or less. Two weeks is the most common choice: long enough to finish meaningful work, short enough to get frequent feedback.

Is the Scrum Master a project manager?

Not exactly. The Scrum Master is accountable for the team's effectiveness and coaches Scrum practice; they don't assign work. Many traditional PM responsibilities are shared among the Product Owner, developers and Scrum Master.

Can Scrum work for non-software teams?

Yes, wherever work can be broken into increments delivered every few weeks — marketing campaigns, content, research and product design teams all use Scrum.
