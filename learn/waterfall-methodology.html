Waterfall Methodology

Waterfall is not dead — it is just often misapplied. Here is when it works, when it doesn't, and how to do it well.

Updated 2026-10-31 · 7 min read · By the Project.Management editorial team

The Waterfall model organises a project into sequential phases — each one completed and approved before the next begins, like water flowing down a series of steps. It is the classic example of a predictive approach: scope, schedule and budget are planned in detail up front, then executed and controlled against that baseline.

Typical Waterfall phases

  1. Requirements — capture and sign off everything the solution must do.
  2. Design — define the architecture and detailed design.
  3. Implementation — build or construct the solution.
  4. Verification — test against requirements.
  5. Deployment — release, hand over and train users.
  6. Maintenance — support and fix issues in operation.

Each phase ends with a formal review, and changes to an approved phase go through change control.

Advantages

  • Predictability: a full schedule and budget early on supports contracts, funding and regulatory approval.
  • Clear documentation: useful for audit, handover and large distributed teams.
  • Simple to understand and manage for stakeholders used to milestone-based reporting.
  • Efficient when change is expensive: it is cheaper to change a drawing than a poured foundation.

Disadvantages

  • Late feedback: users often see the result only at the end, when change is most expensive.
  • Assumes requirements can be known up front — rarely true for new products.
  • Risk accumulates until testing and integration, where surprises cluster.
  • Long time to value — nothing is usable until the final phase.

When Waterfall is the right choice

  • Construction, manufacturing and infrastructure, where work is physical and sequential.
  • Regulated projects requiring full design approval before build.
  • Fixed-price contracts with well-understood scope.
  • Repeat projects where the team has done the same thing many times.
  • Events with an immovable date and a well-known checklist.

Making Waterfall work better

  • Prototype early: show mock-ups during requirements to surface misunderstandings.
  • Use rolling-wave planning: plan the near term in detail and later phases at a higher level.
  • Manage the critical path and protect it with contingency — see the CPM calculator.
  • Track with earned value for objective progress (EVM calculator).
  • Test continuously instead of only at the end.

If several of these "agile-like" practices appeal, you may be heading toward a hybrid approach.
